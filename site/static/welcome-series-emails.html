What a MIC actually is

Subject AWhat a MIC actually is
Subject BMortgages, not stocks
Tag on clickClicked - Education
Jeevan KhunkhunWhat a MIC actually isThe two-minute version I give when people ask what I do.

Hi {First name},

When people ask what I do, I skip the acronym and start with a builder.

Say a builder needs $3 million to finish six homes. The bank can't do the loan, or can't do it fast enough to keep the trades on site. So the builder borrows from a private lender instead, puts the property up as security, and pays interest every month until the homes are sold or refinanced.

A mortgage investment corporation, or MIC, is a pool of loans like that one. Investors buy shares in the MIC. The MIC lends the money out as mortgages. Borrowers pay interest, and the MIC passes that income back to its shareholders as dividends.

A few rules set MICs apart from an ordinary company:

  • As long as it pays out its income, the MIC itself generally pays no corporate tax. The income is taxed once, in the shareholders' hands, as interest.
  • At least half of its assets have to be residential mortgages or cash.
  • It needs at least 20 shareholders, and no single shareholder can own more than 25%.

There are also two things a MIC isn't. It isn't a GIC: there's no CDIC insurance, and your capital isn't guaranteed. And it isn't a stock you can sell on a Tuesday afternoon. You get your money back through the fund's redemption process, which at CORE MIC runs quarterly.

That's the whole idea. The rest of this series is about how we run ours.

Jeevan KhunkhunWhat we look at before we lendIt starts with someone walking the site. Then the real work begins.

Hi {First name},

A big part of my job is deciding which loans we don't make. Before any investor money goes out the door, a loan goes through roughly these steps:

  1. Someone from our team walks the site. What's being built, how far along it is, and whether there's real demand for it once it's finished.
  2. We look hard at the borrower: their track record, their other projects, and how much of their own money is in this one.
  3. We size the loan against an independent appraisal. That's where the loan-to-value ratio comes from.
  4. We structure and approve it. On construction loans, the money is usually advanced in stages as the work gets done.
  5. We manage it until it's paid back.

If you remember one number from this series, make it loan-to-value. If a property appraises at $1,000,000 and we lend $600,000, the loan-to-value is 60%. The other 40% is the cushion: roughly how much value would have to disappear before the loan itself is at risk.

As of August 2026, CORE MIC looked like this:

  • 100% first mortgages, so the fund ranks ahead of any other mortgage on each property
  • A weighted average loan-to-value of 60.57%
  • Almost three quarters of the portfolio (73%) at 65% loan-to-value or lower
  • Lending focused on BC and Alberta

Jeevan KhunkhunWhat happens when a borrower stops payingIt's the right question to ask. Here's the plain answer.

Hi {First name},

If you're thinking about lending money, the most useful thing to ask is what happens when someone doesn't pay it back. So let me answer it plainly.

It happens. In more than thirty years, CareVest has seen loans run late and projects stall. This is roughly what follows.

We usually see it coming. Construction loans are advanced in stages, so we're watching costs and progress the whole way through.

We talk first. Most problems are timing problems: a sale that's slower than planned, or a refinance that takes longer. Often the sensible answer is a short extension, on terms that protect the fund.

If that doesn't work, the borrower sells or refinances. Someone with real equity in a project has every reason to protect it.

Enforcement comes last. In BC and Alberta it goes through the courts, and it takes time. This is where being in first position counts. The fund's mortgage ranks ahead of any other mortgage on the property, and the borrower's equity sits behind it.

It's also where loan-to-value earns its keep. At 60% loan-to-value, the property would have to lose roughly 40% of its appraised value before the loan itself was underwater. That isn't a promise. Legal costs and a soft market both eat into that cushion, and timing can stretch.

I'd rather you hear the rest from me now. CORE MIC is a private investment with real risk. Your capital isn't guaranteed or insured, past returns don't promise future ones, and the Offering Memorandum sets out the risks in full.

If there's a question I haven't answered, just reply. It goes to our team, and a Dealing Representative can walk you through anything specific to your situation.

A MIC is not a GIC

Jeevan KhunkhunA MIC is not a GICThe trade-offs in plain terms, and who it tends to suit.

Hi {First name},

Some people shouldn't invest in CORE MIC, and part of this email is about who. First, how it compares with what's probably already in your portfolio:

Against a GIC. A GIC at a bank is covered by CDIC insurance up to its limits, and your capital is guaranteed. CORE MIC has neither.

Against a bond fund. You can sell a bond fund on any business day. CORE MIC doesn't trade on a market, so there's no daily price to watch, but you can only redeem quarterly, under the notice and other terms in the Offering Memorandum.

Against a REIT. A REIT owns property, so you share in property values going up and down. A MIC lends against property instead. Your return comes from interest, not appreciation, and as a first mortgage lender the fund ranks ahead of the owner's equity.

It's not for you if:

  • you might need the money back on short notice
  • you can't afford to lose some of your capital
  • you're thinking of putting most of your savings in one place

It tends to suit people who want monthly income or steady compounding, who have money they can leave in place for a few years, and who like knowing what's behind their investment.

Whether that describes you is a conversation for you and a Dealing Representative. I can't decide it in an email.
